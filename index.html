About MJC

Mahjong Coach (MJC) introduces beginning and intermediate Mahjong players to Six Box Theory™ (6BT) and helps them put it into practice to understand hand structure and make better playing decisions.

MJC reveals the structure hidden within a Mahjong hand.

Built on Six Box Theory™ (6BT), originally developed for Filipino and Taiwanese 16-Tile Mahjong, MJC will also bring this approach to Chinese 13-Tile Mahjong in an upcoming adaptation.

  5. Review your settings and tiles: Make sure everything is correct.
  6. Press Play On! MJC will organize your hand according to Six Box Theory™.

About 6BT

Six Box Theory™ (6BT) provides a method for understanding Mahjong hand structure.

Originally developed for 16-Tile Mahjong styles such as Filipino and Taiwanese, 6BT uses five meld boxes and one Eye box to explain the structure of a completed hand. An adaptation for Chinese 13-Tile Mahjong, using four meld boxes and one Eye box, is coming soon.

Rather than viewing a hand as individual tiles, 6BT organizes it into boxes, offering a simpler way to visualize, understand, and develop your hand.

This makes it easier to recognize strengths and weaknesses, improve tile efficiency, and make better discard decisions.

Understanding the Boxes

To help you better see the relationships between the tiles in your hand, Six Box Theory™ organizes your hand into boxes.

Complete Boxes (CB)
Completed melds that require no additional tiles. These appear at the bottom of the display.

Developing Boxes (DB)
Groups of tiles that are working toward becoming Complete Boxes. These tile groups are called Candidates. These appear in the middle of the display.

Reserves (R)
Tiles not currently assigned to a CB or DB. These appear at the top of the display.

Complete six boxes—five melds and one Eye— and That's Mahjong!

Understanding Pathways

Pathways show how a Developing Box (DB) can advance to a Complete Box.

Green ↓ — Same-Suit Tile
A different numbered tile of the same suit can advance the DB toward completion.

Orange ↓ — Matching Tile
Another copy of a tile already in the DB can advance it toward completion.

Acceptance
The number of tile opportunities that can advance a DB toward completion.

Sources
How a needed tile can enter your hand—through your own draw or a discard you can claim.

Effective Acceptance
The number above each arrow represents the remaining tile opportunities that can advance the Developing Box toward completion through that pathway.

Understanding Release Tile (RT)

Release Tile (RT) lets you free a tile from a Hidden Complete Box to complete another grouping. MJC shows you the resulting structure before anything changes—you decide whether to keep it.

Meet the Candidates

They're called Candidates because each holds the potential to become a Complete Box. As the hand develops, some Candidates will become Complete Boxes while others may change or be replaced.

Pong-Kang Candidate (PKC)
A four-tile Developing Box that can be declared as a Kang or NEWS.

Chow-Pong Candidate (CPC)
Three or four suited tiles that can develop into either a Chow or a Pong.

Eye-Pong Candidate (EPC)
Two matching tiles that can serve as the Eye or develop into a Pong.

Eye Candidate (EC)
Two matching tiles being held as the Eye.

Chow Candidates
Two suited tiles that are working toward becoming a Chow.

Double-Sided Wait (DSW)
Two consecutive suited tiles that can complete a Chow from either end.

Middle Wait (MW)
Two suited tiles with one number between them, waiting for the middle tile to complete the Chow.

Edge Wait (EW)
A 1–2 or 8–9 combination waiting for the single tile that completes the Chow.

Half-Eye (HE)
A single tile being held as a possible half of a pair for the Eye.

5. Reserves

Reserves are tiles outside MJC’s displayed Developing and Complete Boxes. They may later join a grouping as new tiles enter your hand.

A Reserve and a Half Eye may each contain a single tile. Their placement tells you the difference: a Half Eye occupies a Developing Box as a possible Eye, while a Reserve sits outside the displayed boxes.

Example

6. Reading Your Structure

Start with the Complete Boxes. Then look at the Developing Boxes and what each needs. Finally, examine the Reserves. This gives you a practical way to review your hand’s structure.

An Empty Developing Box marks an unfilled structural position. It contains no tiles.

Keep MJC’s record consistent with the declaration and meld you actually play at the table. Use Correct Last Entry or Fix Hand when a recorded action or grouping needs correction.

Understanding Pathways

In this chapter

  • Use Pathways in Your Decisions

Pathways show how supported Developing Boxes can advance toward completion. They help you connect the tiles already grouped in a box with the incoming tiles that could help it.

1. Turn Pathways On

  1. Open your hand in Coaching View.
  2. Tap Pathways: On to show pathway information.
  3. Examine the markers and numbers added to supported Developing Boxes.

Once Pathways are displayed, the button reads Pathways: Off, offering to hide them. Pathways Explained opens a quick reference.

In Standard View, the Pathways control reads Pathways: NA and is unavailable. Switch to Coaching View to use it.

2. Read the Colored Markers

The marker color identifies the kind of incoming tile that advances the grouping.

Green ↓ — Same-Suit Tile
A different numbered tile of the same suit can advance the grouping.

Orange ↓ — Matching Tile
Another copy of a tile already in the grouping can advance it.

For example, adding 3 Bam to 4 Bam · 5 Bam follows a same-suit pathway. Adding Red to Red · Red follows a matching-tile pathway.

The colors identify pathway types. They do not mark a tile for discard.

3. Read the Numbers

Acceptance
The number of tile opportunities that can advance the Developing Box toward completion.

Sources
How a needed tile can enter your hand: through your own draw or through an eligible discard claim. Claim eligibility depends on the grouping and game rules.

Effective Acceptance (EA)
The number above a pathway marker. It represents the remaining tile opportunities MJC calculates for that particular pathway.

Count opportunities, not promises

These figures describe opportunities based on MJC’s recorded information. They do not guarantee that a tile will arrive, identify the next draw, or show a percentage chance of winning.

A tile type and a tile opportunity are different things. A DSW may need either of two tile types, with multiple copies of each type potentially contributing to its opportunities.

6. Use Pathways in Your Decisions

  1. Identify the Developing Box and the tiles it contains.
  2. Read the marker colors to distinguish same-suit and matching-tile pathways.
  3. Identify the incoming tiles that advance each pathway.
  4. Read the EA figures and the Acceptance and Sources summaries.
  5. Consider how the grouping fits the rest of your hand.

7. Target Tiles and the Target Tiles Panel

Target Tiles (TT) are tiles that can help your current Developing Boxes advance or become complete. They give you specific tiles to watch for as you play.

For example, a Double-Sided Wait containing 7 and 8 Bam has two Target Tiles: 6 Bam and 9 Bam. Either can complete a Chow.

Target Tiles

Using the Target Tiles Panel

The Target Tiles Panel (TTP) brings your current targets together in one place. Tap the Target Tiles tab to open the panel. Tap it again to close it.

Your targets can change as your hand develops. Recheck the panel after your groupings change rather than relying on an earlier list.

Tracking Remaining Target Tiles

The number beneath each Target Tile shows how many copies may still be available. MJC automatically accounts for copies already in your hand.

As you watch the table, tap a Target Tile once for each additional copy you see discarded or exposed. Each tap reduces its remaining count by one.

A count of zero appears in red. Tap again after zero to return to the starting count, which already accounts for copies in your hand.

MJC remembers your recorded decrements as Target Tiles change during the hand. Starting a New Hand clears them.

Keep Watching the Table

A Target Tile is useful to your hand, but that does not guarantee that a copy is still available. Watch the discards and exposed melds to help judge your opportunities.

Use the panel as a quick reference, then return your attention to your physical hand and the table.

1. What Release Tile Does

Release Tile (RT) lets you explore a different arrangement by freeing a tile from an eligible hidden Complete Box and using it in another grouping.

The tile stays in your hand. RT changes its structural assignment; it does not draw, claim, or discard a tile.

In this example, moving the 3 Char completes one Chow and leaves 1 Char · 2 Char developing. You still hold exactly the same tiles.

An alternate arrangement is not automatically an improvement. RT lets you inspect the result before deciding whether to keep it.

2. When RT Is Available

Look for RT beside an eligible Complete Box heading in Current Hand Coaching View while preparing to draw or claim.

Eligible boxes are hidden melds. Exposed melds and the Eye do not offer this control. RT is not offered during the ordinary discard step.

The RT Explained link provides a short explanation when Release Tile controls are available.

3. Select a Tile and Grouping

  1. Tap RT beside the hidden Complete Box you want to explore.
  2. In Select Tile to Release, tap the tile you want to move out of that grouping.
  3. If viable alternatives are available, MJC displays Select Alternate Grouping.
  4. Review the listed Developing Boxes and tap the destination grouping.

Each alternate grouping shows its box number, grouping type, and tiles. Choose by those details so you direct the released tile to the intended grouping.

Only the alternatives MJC finds for the selected tile are listed. RT does not let you place a tile into any box arbitrarily.

4. Preview and Confirm

Selecting an alternate grouping opens Preview. The Expected Results area shows the affected groupings in the proposed arrangement.

Before confirming, check:
  • What remains of the original Complete Box?
  • What does the destination grouping become?
  • Does the resulting arrangement support your intended plan?

The preview focuses on affected groupings. It is not a complete redisplay of every unchanged box in your hand.

Tap Confirm to apply the previewed arrangement. MJC returns to the hand display with the updated structure.

Confirming RT does not consume a draw or discard. Continue with the action shown in the hand instruction.

5. Back, Cancel, and No Alternatives

Back
From Preview, returns to the alternate-grouping step. From that step, returns to tile selection. Use it to explore another choice before confirming.

Cancel
Closes the RT process without applying the proposed arrangement.

No viable alternate groupings available
This notice means MJC found no supported destination for the tile you selected. Tap OK to close the notice.

If you want to explore another tile, reopen RT and make a different selection. Finding no alternative for one tile does not mean every tile in that hidden meld has the same result.

Use RT to investigate structure deliberately: inspect the starting grouping, inspect the alternative, then confirm only when you want the displayed result.

MJC’s coaching helps you understand the structure of your recorded hand and the reasons behind a suggested choice. You decide which action to take in your actual game.

1. Read a Discard Recommendation

In Coaching View, MJC can display a discard recommendation when your hand is ready for a discard. This includes the Dealer’s first discard from the Starting Hand Display.

Recommended Discard: White.
Hide Insight

Example recommendation; the suggested tile depends on your recorded hand.

Read the tile name carefully, including its suit where applicable. The recommendation does not discard the tile for you. Select the tile and complete the normal discard confirmation when you decide to use it.

Tap Hide if you want to consider your own choice first. Tap Show Recommended Discards to reveal the recommendation again.

Recommendations are not displayed at every step. They are unavailable in Standard View, during acquisition or replacement draws, and after the hand is complete. The current special-hand pursuits also suppress ordinary discard recommendations.

2. Open Insight

Tap Insight beside the hand-display recommendation to open Why This Discard?

The explanation identifies the recommended tile or choices and describes the structural effect of discarding one. Recommended tile names appear in bold.

Read for the effect

Does the discard remove a Reserve? Does it break a Developing Box? What remains afterward? Which groupings stay intact?

Insight explains the current recommendation. Revisit it after your hand changes rather than assuming an earlier explanation still applies.

3. Understand the Reasoning

When Reserves are available

MJC compares their contribution to developing the hand. Its explanations use Connectivity, then Centricity, to describe the comparison.

Connectivity concerns nearby same-suit support. Centricity concerns a suited tile’s position toward the middle of the suit. Refer to the Glossary for these terms.

Discarding a Reserve leaves the displayed Developing and Complete Boxes intact. That structural effect is part of the explanation.

When there are no Reserves

MJC must consider a tile from an existing developing grouping. Insight explains which grouping changes and what is preserved.

Depending on the structure, MJC may compare Chow Candidates’ completion opportunities, consider removing a tile from a Chow-Pong Candidate, or weigh preserving an Eye Candidate against other developing options.

Some comparisons also take account of the hand’s progress, using your recorded discards. A recommendation can therefore change as play advances even when familiar grouping types remain in the hand.

Read the current explanation for the actual comparison being made. A useful recommendation describes both what you give up and what you retain.

4. Handle Equal Choices

Sometimes MJC gives equal preference to more than one discard. It may list tile names joined by “or”, or summarize a larger set and direct you to Insight.

Possible forms

Recommended Discard: White or East.

Recommended Discard: Any of your 3 Reserves.

These are examples of alternative recommendations, not instructions to discard multiple tiles.

Discard only one tile. Equal preference means MJC’s current assessment does not distinguish those choices. It does not mean they will produce identical future hands.

Open Insight to see the named choices and the reason they are tied.

5. Read Meld Recommendations

When an incoming tile offers multiple meld groupings, MJC compares the structure remaining after each choice and marks its recommendations with MJC Recommended. Several choices may be equally recommended. An unmarked grouping remains available for you to select.

The Affected Developing Boxes display below the choices shows the original DBs involved, alongside the highlighted incoming tile. Use this visual context to understand how the tile connects with your hand. Insight then explains the structure each choice would leave behind.

Tap Insight to explore what each choice leaves in your hand and why MJC favors its recommendations. When choices are tied, the explanation acknowledges that tie. Tap Hide Insight to collapse the explanation.

MJC bases this comparison on your recorded hand. Keep watching the table and use your own judgment when choosing.

A meld recommendation and a discard recommendation address different decisions. Choosing a meld determines the grouping to record. Choosing a discard determines the tile to release afterward, when a discard is required.

Chapter 6 explains the meld-choice and declaration controls.

6. Read Coaching Alerts

Coaching View may display messages that call attention to the current structure or pursuit.

BOLO for Eyes

“BOLO” means “be on the lookout.” This alert draws attention to the need to develop an Eye.

Protect Your Only Eye

Draws attention to your only Eye Candidate. Consider the effect before using its tiles in another grouping.

Over-Paired

Draws attention to an excess of pairs in the current structure. Review how those pairs support your intended hand and whether a special-hand option is relevant.

Pursuing a Special Hand

Messages such as Pursuing Siete Pares or Pursuing Escalera identify the active pursuit. Names may differ with the ruleset, such as Seven Pairs or Straight.

Kang Deferred

Reminds you that a Kang declaration has been postponed. It does not mean the declaration or its replacement draw has already been completed.

Treat alerts as prompts to inspect your hand. They do not replace the current action instruction, declare a meld, or make a discard.

Accurate coaching depends on an accurate recorded hand. If MJC’s tiles or melds no longer match your actual hand, correct the record before relying on its next recommendation.

Use Fix Hand when a broader correction is needed, such as a missing meld, incorrect exposed status, or a mismatch involving several tiles.

First decide what your actual hand is preparing to do:

Preparing to Draw
You have completed your discard and are waiting to acquire again.

Preparing to Discard
You have acquired a tile and still need to discard.

Select the state that matches the actual point in your game. It determines the tile target and the next action after correction.

Use How to Fix Your Hand on the correction screen if you need a quick reminder while working.

5. Correct the Melds

Review the melds listed in Fix Hand before entering the remaining tiles. Compare each grouping with your actual hand.

  • Keep melds that are recorded correctly.
  • Set each meld’s Hidden or Exposed status correctly.
  • Use Remove for a meld that should not remain in the recorded hand.
  • Use Add Meld for a missing grouping.

Removing a meld also removes its tiles from the correction’s recorded melds. Those tiles are not automatically transferred into Free Tiles. If you still hold them, include them in the appropriate corrected grouping or free-tile entry.

Add a Missing Meld

  1. Tap Add Meld.
  2. Choose Chow, Pong, Kang, or NEWS when available.
  3. Select the tiles using the displayed builder controls.
  4. Choose Hidden or Exposed.
  5. Use the builder’s Add control to add the grouping.

For a Chow, choose the suit and the starting tile of the three-tile sequence. For a Pong or Kang, choose the matching tile. NEWS uses the fixed four Winds and is available only when allowed by the ruleset settings.

6. Correct the Free Tiles

Free Tiles are the tiles you hold outside the melds already listed in Fix Hand. Enter each remaining tile with the correct quantity.

Avoid counting tiles twice

If a Pong is already listed in the meld area, do not enter those same three copies again as Free Tiles.

Green entries come from MJC’s last recorded hand and may no longer match your actual hand. Check them rather than assuming they are already correct.

Tap a tile to cycle its count from zero through four. Long-press to clear its count. Work through your actual tiles one group at a time.

Read the tile-count guidance above the Free Tiles section. It accounts for your next-action state and recorded melds, then shows how many Free Tiles are required and any adjustment needed.

Four-tile melds affect the total. Follow the displayed target rather than assuming every corrected hand must contain exactly 16 or 17 physical tiles.

7. Review and Accept

Before accepting, compare the whole correction with your actual hand:

  • The next-action state is correct.
  • Every meld has the correct tiles.
  • Hidden and Exposed statuses are correct.
  • All remaining tiles are entered as Free Tiles.
  • No physical tile is counted twice.
  • The tile total matches the displayed target.

If MJC displays a validation message, resolve the stated issue. Checks include the required count and the limit of four copies of a tile across the recorded hand.

Tap Accept when the correction is valid and matches your actual hand. MJC rebuilds the display from the corrected record.

Tap Cancel if you want to leave Fix Hand without applying the correction.

After accepting, read the hand instruction before resuming. Confirm that MJC is expecting the same next action as your actual game.

There’s a lot to MJC, so before we begin, let me tell you what Mahjong Coach (MJC) is and what it can do for you. MJC is a teaching app designed to introduce beginning and intermediate Mahjong players to Six Box Theory™ (6BT), a method for understanding Mahjong hand structure. By helping you recognize that structure, MJC can help you improve your tile efficiency and make better discard decisions.

Using MJC begins with entering the tiles in your starting hand. As you play, you'll continue entering the tiles you draw, discard, or acquire.

From there, MJC does the organizing— arranging your hand into Complete Boxes, Developing Boxes, and Reserves, revealing its structure, and helping you recognize opportunities.

You make the decisions; MJC helps you understand them.

MJC currently supports Filipino and Taiwanese 16-tile Mahjong. An adaptation for Chinese 13-tile Mahjong is coming soon.

Six Box Theory™ began with 16-tile Mahjong. A regular winning hand contains five melds and a pair—six boxes. That’s where the name comes from. The Chinese 13-tile adaptation will use four melds and a pair—five boxes, applying the same approach to understanding your hand.

How much of Mahjong is luck? How much is skill?

According to our highly scientific PFA method, precisely 68.74% of winning at Mahjong is luck. That leaves 31.26% for skill!

Disclaimer: PFA stands for "Plucked From Air." We made those numbers up!

But seriously, here's something we believe:

Luck is the tiles you get. Skill is what you do with them.

Give Yourself Time

And developing that skill takes time—and practice.

You’re learning to see your hand through the lens of 6BT while also getting used to recording your tiles in MJC. That’s a lot to take in at once! Give yourself room to practice. You don’t have to understand everything in your first hand.

I recommend starting with relaxed practice games, ideally with others who are learning Mahjong or getting familiar with MJC too. Give yourselves permission to pause, look at your hands, and talk through a decision. MJC isn’t designed for tournament play or the pace of experienced players racing through a hand.

There’s no prize here for being the fastest person to discard a tile.

MJC Only Knows What You Tell It

I get it. Entering your starting tiles, then recording every draw, claim, and discard can feel like a hassle at first. But that’s how MJC knows what’s in your hand. It can only work with what you tell it.

If you draw a 5 Bam but record a 5 Dot, MJC will faithfully help you understand a hand you don’t actually have. So keep its record in step with your real tiles. If you make an entry mistake, use the correction tools. That’s what they’re there for.

With practice, recording becomes more familiar, and so does recognizing your hand’s structure. Give yourself time to get comfortable with both.

Keep Watching the Table

This is something I’ll remind you of throughout: keep watching the table.

MJC knows your recorded hand—what you have and what you need. But only you can see what’s happening around you. What are the other players discarding? What are they claiming? Which melds have they exposed?

Those things matter. Let MJC help you understand your hand, but remember to look up from the screen. There’s a whole game happening out there.

Better Decisions, One Hand at a Time

MJC can’t guarantee you’ll win every hand. Luck has plenty to say about Mahjong. You can make a thoughtful decision and still not win.

What MJC can do is help you see your hand more clearly and understand the choices in front of you. As you become familiar with 6BT, the goal is for you to start recognizing those possibilities yourself.

So take your time. Ask questions. Try things. Every hand is an opportunity to learn.

Every Tile Has a Role

Then there are the tiles that don’t fit into the displayed boxes yet. MJC places them in Reserves, for now.

“For now” is worth remembering. A Reserve may find a partner as the game progresses and help develop your hand. Or it may become a useful discard choice.

The key is that every tile has a role. Some help you complete a grouping. Others may be better discarded to preserve the groupings you’re building.

This is the Principle of Contribution: a tile’s value is shaped by what it contributes to the hand as a whole. That contribution is dynamic: a tile’s role can change as the hand develops.

So when you look at your hand, try asking more than “What tiles do I have?” Ask, “What are these tiles doing for my hand?”

Which groupings are complete? Which are developing? What does each one need? And which tiles are still waiting for a job?

First, What Changed?

Before reaching for a discard, look at the updated structure. What did that incoming tile do for your hand?

Perhaps it completed a Chow. Perhaps it gave a Reserve a partner. Perhaps it created two possibilities, and now you have to choose what you want to preserve.

Start with the boxes. Which are complete? Which are still developing? Do you have an Eye Candidate? What does each developing grouping need?

Pathways can help you examine the supported groupings. Look at their Target Tiles—the tiles that can advance or complete a Developing Box—and the opportunities to acquire them. You don’t have to calculate everything in your head. That’s part of what MJC is helping you visualize.

What Can You Let Go?

Now look at your Reserves. Is there a tile you can release while keeping your developing groupings intact?

Remember what we said earlier: every tile has a role. Sometimes that role is helping complete a grouping. Sometimes it's being a tile you can discard while preserving something useful.

A useful question

“If I discard this tile, what am I keeping, and what am I giving up?”

Don’t assume every Reserve is equally useful. One may have nearby same-suit support; another may be isolated. MJC considers those differences when comparing its discard choices.

And what if there are no Reserves? Then a Developing Box may have to change. Consider how discarding a tile from that box affects your hand’s structure.

Ask MJC Why

Read the Recommended Discard, then open Insight. Think of it as asking, “All right, MJC. Why that tile?”

Look for what the explanation says will remain intact, which grouping changes, and what possibilities the hand retains.

Here comes that reminder: keep watching the table.

MJC knows your recorded hand. It doesn’t record every opponent’s ordinary discard or exposed meld in this workflow. Those observations come from you.

In your hand:

There are four copies of 5 Bam in the set. You hold two. You can see the other two on the table: one in the discard pile and one in an opponent’s exposed meld.

How many copies remain available to complete your Pong? Zero.

Your pair can still serve as an Eye. Whether you keep it depends on the rest of your hand.

Whether tile availability is tracked by you or recorded in a tool, its usefulness depends on accurate observation and entry. MJC helps you read your hand; you keep reading the table.

What have other players been discarding? What have they claimed? What melds can you see? Has someone’s play given you a reason to reconsider the tile you were about to release?

MJC’s structural recommendation is not a promise that a tile is safe to discard. A tile that contributes little to your hand might still be useful to someone else.

And seeing an exposed meld doesn’t reveal everything in that player’s hidden hand. Observe, think, and make the best decision you can with the information available.

This is where your judgment and MJC’s help come together. Let the app help you understand your hand while you stay aware of the game around it.

Mahjong skill isn’t measured by the speed of your discard, but by the quality of your decisions.

Give yourself time to understand the choice. Read Insight, consider what you see at the table, then decide. You may agree with MJC’s recommendation or choose differently. What matters is understanding your reason.

Make Your Choice

Once you’ve decided, select the tile in MJC and check the discard confirmation carefully. Make sure the tile you record is the tile you actually release at the table.

Then settle your rack when practical and return your attention to the game. You don’t need to keep debating the same choice while the next player is taking a turn.

If the decision was interesting, save the hand for later. A transcript gives you something concrete to discuss when there’s more time.

And if the hand doesn’t turn out the way you hoped, that doesn’t automatically make the discard a bad one. Remember, luck gets a turn too.

What matters for learning is being able to say, “Here’s what I saw, here’s what I was trying to keep, and here’s why I made that choice.”

That’s a lot more useful than “I tapped the tile MJC told me to.”

Winning feels good. I’m certainly not going to argue with that. But it isn’t the only sign that you’re improving.

Maybe you recognized a Double-Sided Wait without checking the label. Maybe you noticed that your only pair needed protecting. Maybe you could explain your discard instead of just hoping it was right.

Those are things you’re learning. Give yourself credit for them.

You can make a thoughtful decision and still not win. You can also win a hand without fully understanding why it worked. The useful question afterward is, “What did I learn?”

Welcome to Our Table

If you’ve joined us for Mahjong, you’ve probably heard “Cha na lang!” before the tiles have even settled. Welcome to JEZEMS.

This guide shows you how we set up our table and get a hand underway. We play Filipino 16-tile Mahjong with our own table conventions. Other groups may do things differently, so if you’ve played elsewhere, a few details might be new to you.

And if you’re just learning, that’s fine too. Ask questions. We’ve all had to learn where to sit, how many tiles to take, and what to do when a flower turns up.

We’ll walk through it together, with pictures along the way. First, let’s get the table ready.

Let’s Get Set Up

Let’s Get Set Up

Before we start admiring our tiles, let’s get the table ready. Everyone has a part in this—and a little attention now makes everything that follows easier.

1. What Goes on the Table?

You’ll need a table, four chairs, and a mat in the middle. Our mat has Wind markings already. Yours doesn’t have to; we’ll explain how to handle that when we choose seats.

Our Equipment Checklist
  • 144 Mahjong tiles: 108 suit tiles, 28 honor tiles, and 8 Flowers and Seasons.
  • Three dice.
  • Four separate seating tiles: East, South, West, and North.
  • Four wooden pushers.
  • A center mat.

We call the eight Flowers and Seasons simply flowers. Our seating tiles are separate from the 144 tiles used in the game.

No chips, counters, or separate dealer marker are needed at our table. We play for enjoyment and camaraderie.

The pushers and separate seating tiles are part of how we play at JEZEMS. They’re useful, but you don’t need those particular accessories to play Mahjong.

Our extra-large tiles measure approximately 1 5/12 inches tall × 1 1/8 inches wide × 7/8 inch thick (3.6 × 2.8 × 2.2 cm). Their size helps explain the dimensions of our pushers.

2. Meet the Pushers

Ours are plain wooden bars, 1½ inches square and approximately 21½ inches long. We bought square wooden stock and cut it to the length we wanted. That leaves room at both ends of an 18-stack wall for our fingers to grip the pusher when moving the tiles. Nothing fancy—and very useful.

First, the pushers help us build and move the walls. Later, we bring them back in front of us to support our hands. The tiles rest on the table, leaning upright with their backs against the wood and faces toward us.

3. Wash, Stack, and Build

Take a seat for now. We’ll establish the official seats after building the Great Wall.

Put all 144 tiles face down on the mat and mix them thoroughly. That’s the wash. Each player has a pusher on the mat, ready to serve as a backstop.

Build your wall against your pusher: 18 stacks, two tiles high. Keep the tiles face down, with their bottom edges against the wood. That gives each player a wall of 36 tiles.

Once all four walls are ready, use the pushers to move them forward into a square in the middle. There it is: the Great Wall. Four walls, 36 tiles each—144 altogether.

Now pull your pusher back in front of you. Its next job is to support the starting tiles you’ll receive.

4. Who Sits Where?

Shuffle the four separate seating Wind tiles face down in the middle. Everyone picks one, in no particular order. No dice roll is needed.

We use one of two seating methods. Agree on which one you’re using before the tiles are revealed.

Everyone Moves

Each player moves to the seat matching the Wind tile they drew. Our mat marks the four positions. If yours doesn’t, the house designates the physical East seat, and the other Winds follow counterclockwise.

No One Moves

Whoever draws East establishes East where they’re already sitting, regardless of the mat’s markings. Everyone stays put. The other seats are assigned relative to East, rather than by the tiles those players picked.

East is our first dealer. We draw seating tiles only once, at the start of the game. The deal passes after every hand, with no dealer repeats. Four hands make a round; four rounds make a full game. Later seat changes follow our between-round rotation.

5. Two Throws, Two Jobs

East throws all three dice twice. Keep the two totals separate: the first chooses whose wall; the second chooses where to break it.

First throw: 7

Start with East as 1 and count counterclockwise: East, South, West, North, East, South, West. We’ll break West’s wall.

Second throw: 12

West counts 12 double stacks from West’s own right. Count stacks, not individual tiles. The break is after stack 12, between stacks 12 and 13.

6. Mark the Flower Wall

In our example, West lifts both tiles from stack 12 and places them on stack 11. That creates a four-tile-high tail marking the Flower Wall.

The Playing Wall supplies the starting hands and ordinary draws. The Flower Wall supplies flower replacements. We’ll follow both ends carefully in the next chapter.

Pro Tip: Have MJC Ready

Set your Ruleset and Display order before the wash and dealing begin. Confirm your Role once seating establishes who is East. Save tile entry until your flowers have been fully exchanged. Right now, keep your attention on the table.

The walls are ready, everyone has a seat, and we know where to begin. Now let’s get those tiles into everyone’s hands.

Getting Your Tiles Ready

Getting Your Tiles Ready

The Great Wall is built, the seats are settled, and the wall is broken. Now comes the part where a little teamwork makes everything run smoothly.

Eight Tiles at a Time

The dealer always receives the first eight tiles. It doesn’t matter whose wall was broken or who is giving service. Distribution begins with the dealer and continues counterclockwise. In our opening hand, East is the dealer.

Let’s stay with our example: West’s wall was selected, and stack 12 was moved onto stack 11. The Playing Wall begins at stack 13.

West takes four double stacks—eight tiles—from the start of the Playing Wall and passes them to East. The next eight go to South, then West, then North. Repeat that sequence once more, until everyone has 16 tiles.

Take tiles from the Playing Wall clockwise.
Starting at the break, take four double stacks together, then continue clockwise along the wall for the next group.

Deliver tiles to the players counterclockwise, starting with the dealer.
In our opening hand: East → South → West → North. Repeat once so everyone receives 16 tiles.

A Little Service Goes a Long Way

West won’t necessarily distribute every delivery. As the start of the Playing Wall moves in front of another player, that player continues handing out the tiles. We call this giving service.

It’s easy to get absorbed in arranging your own tiles. We’ve all done it. Just keep an eye on the wall, too. If the next tiles are in front of you, someone may be waiting for your help.

Once everyone has 16 tiles, the player closest to the new start of the Playing Wall uses their pusher to move it forward and angle its starting end toward the middle of the mat. Make it easy for everyone to reach.

Flowers to the Left

As your deliveries arrive, pull out any flowers and place them face up on the table, in front of and to the left of your pusher. Remember, we call all eight flower and season tiles “flowers.”

Call out how many you have: “Two flowers!” That lets the dealer know you’ll need two replacement tiles.

The Dealer Handles the Exchanges

East manages the initial flower exchange, starting with their own flowers and then proceeding counter-clockwise around the table.

Replacement tiles come from the Flower Wall, starting at its raised tail. Take the top tile first, then work down through that stack before moving to the next stack.

After those four tiles, take the top tile from the next double stack. When only its bottom tile remains, move that tile onto the following double stack. That makes a raised tail of three tiles. The tail doesn’t have to stay four tiles high.

If your replacement is another flower, put it with your other flowers and say so. It needs replacing, too.

The dealer waits while you check your replacements. Once you have playing tiles in place of all your flowers, the dealer moves on to the next player who needs an exchange.

One More Tile for the Dealer

Everyone begins with 16 playing tiles, but East needs 17 to make the first discard.

The easiest time for East to take that extra tile is after the flower exchanges are complete. East takes the top tile from the start of the Playing Wall and announces it: “I’m taking my 17th tile.”

East can also take it during the flower exchange, as long as everyone knows. It’s a separate step from taking flower replacements.

A useful little clue

Once East has taken that top tile, the start of the Playing Wall has just its bottom tile showing. That helps everyone see that the dealer’s extra tile has already been taken.

If East’s extra tile is a flower, expose it and replace it from the Flower Wall. Keep replacing any further flowers until East has 17 playing tiles.

Using MJC? Now Enter Your Tiles

Wait until all your flowers have been exchanged before entering your starting tiles into MJC. Get your physical hand settled first, then record it.

Players enter 16 tiles. The dealer enters 17. Check that MJC’s Role matches your seat, and review the tiles against your actual hand.

And give the dealer a little breathing room. East has been handling flower exchanges and is also expected to make the first discard. Entering a starting hand takes a moment, especially when you’re learning.

That’s one reason I recommend friendly practice games while you get comfortable with MJC. We’re here to learn together. A little patience belongs at the table, too.

Ready? Let’s Play

East checks that the flower exchanges are finished and everyone is ready. The hand begins with the dealer’s first discard.

Say every discard aloud. At our JEZEMS table, everyone announces the tile they discard—for example, “7 Bam” or “Red Dragon.” Say it clearly as you place the tile face up in the discard area. This helps everyone follow the game and recognize tiles they may want to claim.

Didn’t catch the announcement, or can’t quite see the tile? It’s always okay to ask, “What was discarded?” Some players rely more on hearing than sight. Repeat the tile’s name clearly when asked. We want everyone to be able to follow along.

From there, we follow our usual draw, claim, and discard cycle. You’ll hear some JEZEMS table terms along the way, including Secret for a hidden Kang and Sagasa when upgrading an exposed Pong with the fourth tile. We also allow NEWS, a grouping containing North, East, West, and South.

Changing Seats Between Rounds

We draw seating tiles only once, at the start of a full game. After that, we follow a set rotation.

After each hand, the deal passes counterclockwise to the next player, with no dealer repeats. After four hands, the round is complete and everyone changes seats.

A full game has four rounds: East, South, West, and North. The player in the East seat starts the dealing for each new round.

You don’t have to play all four rounds. Sometimes we stop after three when we’re ready to call it a night. Play as long as everyone is enjoying it— the remaining round can wait!

You don’t have to remember every detail on your first visit. Ask questions, watch the table, and lend a hand when it’s time to give service.

The tiles are ready. So are we. Cha na lang! Play On!
